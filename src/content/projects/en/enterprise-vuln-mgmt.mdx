## Context

Vulnerabilities arrived from four scanners — Nessus, Snyk, SentinelOne and AWS Inspector — into separate inboxes, with no common prioritization, no SLA and no shared accountability. Security raised findings, engineering didn't know which ones mattered, and remediation drifted for months. Vulnerability management happened in Jira tickets and ad hoc Slack threads, invisible to the leaders who had to choose between fixing, accepting the risk or deprioritizing.

- **Four inboxes** — each scanner reported on its own, and nobody had a single source of truth.
- **No prioritization** — critical issues were mixed with noise, ranked by raw scanner severity at best.
- **No SLA or owner** — findings had no deadline and no named team.
- **No forum** — Heads of technology never reviewed findings together, so risk decisions were made informally or not at all.

## What I did

### Centralized intake

The four scanners feed DefectDojo, which aggregates and normalizes their findings. Each finding is risk-scored and then routed into one Jira workflow: Backlog → Refinement → In progress → Retest → Done. Pentest findings on critical assets enter the same workflow.

### Risk-based prioritization

Severity is not a copy of CVSS. Before anything reaches engineering, the score combines three signals:

- **Asset criticality** — internet-facing? holds customer data? part of the payment path? A single asset attribute can move a finding two tiers either way.
- **Exploitability** — public PoC? exploit framework module? known active campaigns? Sourced from CISA KEV, vendor advisories and threat-intel feeds.
- **Business impact** — what breaks if this is exploited: revenue, data class, regulatory exposure, recovery cost.

Two illustrative findings:

- **Internet-facing API · SQL injection with a public PoC** — criticality *max* × exploitability *max* × impact *max* = **P0, 7-day SLA**. The owner is identified automatically from the service tag in Jira, and a Jira automation rule escalates a missed SLA.
- **Internal admin tool · CVE in a transitive dependency with no execution path** — criticality *low* × exploitability *low* (not reachable) × impact *low* = **P3, backlog**. Reviewed quarterly, blocking nothing.

The same scoring model put them two tiers apart: a 7-day SLA and the quarterly backlog. Context, not the raw score, set the order of work.

### The monthly Vulnerability Forum

This is the half of the program that doesn't fit on a Jira board. I owned it end to end: I wrote the playbook, set the agenda and presented the dashboard to the CTO, the Heads and coordinators of the technology teams, and the people responsible for the areas that owned the vulnerabilities. The forum is where prioritization, SLA tracking and risk-acceptance decisions happen with the people who can authorize them.

- **Cadence** — monthly, a standing slot on the calendars of the Heads of every technology team.
- **Pre-read 48 hours before**, so the meeting was for decisions, not status.
- **Risk-acceptance track** — when a fix wasn't viable (end-of-life legacy system, unsupported vendor, refactor cost higher than the risk), the finding moved to a structured acceptance track. The asset owner and the Head of the relevant team co-signed, compensating controls were documented (WAF rule, network segmentation, extra monitoring), and the acceptance had a review date (90, 180 or 365 days) when it came back to the forum.

```mermaid
    D -->|P3 / P4| G[Backlog · reviewed quarterly]

    E --> H[Engineering owner]
    F --> H

    H --> I{Fix viable?}
    I -->|Yes| J[Fix · retest · close]
    I -->|No| K[Risk acceptance<br/>asset owner + Head sign-off<br/>compensating controls]

    J --> L[Monthly Vulnerability Forum]
    K --> L
    G --> L

    L --> M[Heads of technology teams]
    L --> N[SLA dashboard · top findings · blockers]
    L --> O[Policy and roadmap decisions]
```

Four scanners converge in DefectDojo, every finding is scored on context and lands in Jira with an SLA tier, and everything — fixed, accepted or backlogged — comes back to the monthly forum where the Heads decide.

## Business value

<Callout kind="value" lang="en">
The company got one place to see its vulnerability exposure and one meeting where the people accountable for it made decisions. Engineering worked on what was most likely to hurt the business first, instead of trying to fix everything at once. And when someone asked "why isn't this fixed?", the answer was no longer "we didn't know" but "we know, here is the sign-off, here are the controls, here is the review date" — a defensible position for audits, customers and leadership.
</Callout>

## Lessons

- **A vulnerability program is a rhythm, not software.** Tools normalize data; the forum makes decisions; the playbook makes the forum useful. Without the rhythm, the tools become a graveyard.
- **CVSS is not severity.** Asset criticality and exploitability move a finding by tiers, as the P0 API and the P3 admin tool show.
- **Risk acceptance is a feature, not a failure.** A governed path with sign-off, controls and a review date replaced the informal, invisible version.
- **Build the forum for the people who decide.** Engineers fix; Heads decide what gets fixed when, so the agenda and the pre-read were written for them.
- **Pentests belong in the same workflow.** Routing pentest and scanner findings through the same Jira flow is how the company stops rediscovering the same gaps.
